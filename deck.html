Why data-driven security

Detection is a data problem first, a model problem second

Real access, no malware

About 82% of detections in 2025 had no malware. Attackers use real passwords and trusted paths, so the clue is unusual behaviour in context.

Attacks before the fix

Mandiant estimates attackers exploit some weaknesses about 7 days before a fix exists. Defenders must predict which weaknesses to fix first.

What good data provides

  • Visibility: record what you need to detect
  • Context: understand who, what and how exposed
  • Feedback: learn from each incident
Sources: CrowdStrike Global Threat Report 2026; Mandiant M-Trends 2026 (via Google Cloud blog).

The data-to-decision path

From raw events to decisions

Telemetry Collect events from endpoints, identities, network and cloud.
Data engineering Normalize records, enrich context and check quality.
Baselines Learn what is normal for this user, device or service.
Detection Combine rules, machine learning and threat intelligence.
Decision Choose a human, AI-assisted or automated response.
Feedback Record outcomes so detections and models improve.
Key idea: A model is only one part of the path. Poor data quality can weaken every decision that follows.

Cybersecurity data engineering

A detection pipeline needs quality checks at every stage

Treat data health like device health. Raise an alert when a log source goes quiet, just as you do for a bad process.
Detection as code: keep detections in version control, test them, review them, and link them to MITRE ATT&CK. Measure coverage.

The human factor

People are the weak link, but also our best sensors

62%

of breaches involve the human element: mistakes, misuse, stolen passwords and social engineering.

Verizon DBIR 2026. The definition changed in 2024, so do not compare it directly with 85% in 2021.
Social engineering is moving to pretexting: the attacker pretends to be someone trusted, inside a real conversation or process. There are fewer obvious fake emails.

Target

Attackers go where trust is: leaders, finance, help desk, suppliers.

Sensor

Reports and doubts are signals. Report rate and time to report can be measured.

Decision-maker

People have judgement, context and responsibility. Models do not.

How data helps people

  • Baselines show what is normal for each person and group
  • Risk-based checks: extra checks only when risk is high
  • Measured training: look at behaviour and reporting, not certificates
  • Reporting without blame keeps the sensor network alive

Case 1 · AI-driven defence wins

Big Sleep: an AI agent finds a bug before attackers do

What happened

  • Oct 2024: Google Project Zero and DeepMind's Big Sleep agent found a serious memory bug in SQLite (a stack buffer underflow)
  • It was reported and fixed on the same day, in a development version, before the official release
  • 2025: Google said Big Sleep helped stop the use of CVE-2025-6965 just before attackers could exploit it

Where people stayed involved

  • Researchers started the search from an old, fixed bug (variant analysis)
  • People checked the result, reported the bug and owned the fix process

Why defence won

  • Speed and scale: AI read code faster than any analyst
  • Early action: found before release
  • Ready process: maintainers could fix it quickly
Warning

The bug was in a development version, and the claims come from the vendor. The lesson is the pattern, not the hype.

Sources: Google Project Zero, "From Naptime to Big Sleep" (Oct 2024); Digital Trends report on CVE-2025-6965 (2025).

Case 2 · AI-powered attackers win

Seeing and hearing is no longer proof of who someone is

Arup, Hong Kong (Jan 2024)

  • A finance employee joined a video call with a deepfake CFO and colleagues
  • HK$200M (about US$25.6M) was sent in 15 transfers to 5 accounts
  • The attackers reportedly used public audio and video

What failed: trust in what people see and hear. No independent check of an unusual payment.

AI-run spy campaign (Sept 2025)

  • Anthropic found and stopped a campaign by a Chinese state-sponsored group (GTG-1002)
  • About 30 targets. AI did most of the technical work. People made the key decisions
  • It shows that AI lowers the skill needed for complex attacks

What decided it: speed, many tasks at once, and detection by the AI provider, not by the targets.

+89% more operations by attackers who use AI, in one year (CrowdStrike 2026).
Defender gap: no data about business processes such as payments and approvals. Only data about computers.
Sources: CNN and Hong Kong police reports on Arup; Anthropic, "Disrupting the first reported AI-orchestrated cyber espionage campaign" (Nov 2025); CrowdStrike 2026.

Adversarial resilience

When the defender's AI is the target

Adversarial resilience

Resilience has many layers. It is not one feature of the model

Data layer
Check the origin and integrity of training data and feedback labels. Set data quality targets. Keep unverified sources separate.
Model layer
Robust training, calibration, tests of old versus new models, and monitoring of drift and performance on hidden and adversarial test sets.
System layer
Levels of autonomy, a kill switch and rollback, minimum rights for agents, rate limits, and different detectors (rules + ML + intelligence).
Human layer
Power to override, structured review of unclear cases, reporting without blame, purple-team and incident exercises.
Governance
Inventory of models, audit trails, resilience metrics, and red-team tests of AI parts before and after launch.
Test question: if an attacker controlled 5% of your training data, or hid instructions in an email your AI assistant reads, what would break first? Who would notice?

Read an alert: score, threshold and two kinds of mistake

Score and threshold

A model gives each event a score from 0 to 1. A higher score means more unusual. A score is a clue, not proof. The threshold is the line where you raise an alert.

Two kinds of mistake

  • False alarm (false positive): an alert, but no attack. It wastes time and trust.
  • Missed attack (false negative): an attack, but no alert. It costs money and data.
In the simulation, a score like 0.91 is a strong clue, but you still ask for context. If data is missing, the score can be too low.

Before you act: get context, then choose how wide to contain

Six context questions

  1. Who? The user, their role and their normal work
  2. What? The system, and how important it is
  3. Where? Is the place or network normal?
  4. When? Is the time normal?
  5. Compare: What do similar users or devices do?
  6. What else? Other computers, accounts or tokens with the same clue
What's yours?: identity records, asset lists and past logs.

Simulation toolkit · 5 of 6

Five questions to ask, four meters to watch

1
How sure am I?
Look at the score and the evidence. Add context if you can.
2
What if I am wrong?
Compare the cost of a false alarm with the cost of a miss.
3
Can I undo it?
Easy to undo: act faster. Hard to undo: ask a person.
4
Who is affected?
One account, one team or the whole campus? Choose the smallest scope that works.
5
What will we learn?
Save a label, a note or a rule so the next time is faster.

Visibility

▲ add context, fix data, hunt widely

▼ safe lists, ignore data gaps

Speed

▲ act early, automate safe steps

▼ wait, long queues

Trust

▲ clear talk, small actions, no blame

▼ blunt controls, surprise lockouts

Integrity

▲ check data and labels, lasting fixes

▼ unchecked retraining, narrow fixes

How the story ends: the ending depends on the average of the four meters. Speed without trust loses. Trust without visibility loses too. Questions 1 to 3 also tell you the right level on the autonomy dial.

Key words · 1 of 2

Data and detection, in simple English

  • Telemetry: records that systems send about what they do
  • Log: a written record of events on a system
  • Baseline: what is normal for a user or device
  • Anomaly: something unusual
  • Score: a number from 0 to 1. Higher means more unusual
  • Threshold: the score line where an alert is raised
  • Context: extra facts about who, what, where and when (also called enrichment)
  • True positive: a real attack that raised an alert
  • False positive: an alert when there is no attack (a false alarm)
  • False negative: an attack that raised no alert (a miss)
  • Label: a saved verdict, real or false alarm, so the model can learn
  • Drift: a model slowly becomes less accurate as data changes
  • Dwell time: how long an attacker stays hidden
  • Breakout time: time from first access to spreading to another system

Key words · 2 of 2

Attacks and decisions, in simple English

  • Phishing: a fake message that tricks you
  • Deepfake: fake video or voice made by AI
  • Beacon: a small signal sent again and again to an attacker
  • Password spray: trying a few common passwords on many accounts
  • MFA: a second proof of identity, besides the password
  • MFA fatigue: many approval requests, hoping the user taps "approve"
  • Poisoning: an attacker adds bad data to teach the model wrong things
  • SOC: the team that watches for attacks
  • SOAR: tools that run response steps automatically
  • Containment: stopping the attacker from spreading or acting
  • Safe list (allowlist): things you choose not to alert on. A risky shortcut
  • Check through another channel: confirm a request by a different route, such as a phone call to a known number
  • Two-person approval: a second person must approve before money moves
  • Human in the loop: a person checks or approves the AI's work
